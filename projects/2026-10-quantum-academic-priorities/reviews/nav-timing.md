# Navigation and timing: critical academic review

Search date: 12 October 2026. Window: 1 January 2016–12 October 2026, with latest-five-year emphasis. Twenty actual papers screened; six full-text primary papers reviewed. Eighteen queries and decisions are recorded in `nav-timing.json`. Full HTML and readable extracts are retained in `nav-docs/`. Searches covered transportable clocks, hybrid inertial sensing, field magnetic/gravity navigation, early 2016 foundations and independent updates. This is a bounded critical scan, not a systematic review. Abstract-screened papers are discovery context, not methodological evidence.

Early work was retained in screening: Battelier et al. (2016), Cheiney et al. (2018), Grotti et al. (2018), and marine gravimetry (2018). No date quota was imposed. An August 2016 point-source-interferometer lead could not be opened at the attempted publisher URL and is not a logged source. Published airborne gravity comparisons and the 2025 open-data update are useful follow-up candidates, particularly for competitive classical baselines; neither is counted as a deep review here. Search found an independent 2026 navigation review, but no opened, specific experimental rebuttal or independent replication of the two Q-CTRL navigation studies. This is an access/search result, not proof that none exists.

## S10 — Optical clocks at sea

Roslund et al.; Nature, 24 April 2024; preprint 23 August 2023. Version of record, full HTML; T1. [Original](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07225-2).

The contribution is a system demonstration: an iodine spectrometer, frequency comb and controller provide useful optical and microwave timing on a moving ship. At NIST, comparisons use a maser and composite timescale; at sea, three co-located clocks are compared with one another. The 35 L package excludes the external supply and laptop. The strongest directly measured ship result is the PICKLES–EPIC pair's 8×10^-15 instability at 100,000 s, equivalent to roughly 400 ps daily holdover without drift correction. [S10 Main; Figs.1–3; Methods Clock comparison]

The measurement boundary matters. Long-time individual performance is inferred from pairwise data, assuming equal contributions; no independent high-performance reference was available at sea. The authors acknowledge potential environmental correlations. The container was air-conditioned, with residual temperature cycles; one clock's external power supply required restart. Raw and dedrifted laboratory curves differ substantially at long integration times. Data and analysis routines require requests. The authors declare no competing interests; Vector Atomic affiliations nevertheless make commercial participation transparent. [S10 Main; Methods; Extended Data Fig.4; Data/Code availability; Author information]

*Analysis:* This is strong evidence for practical holdover and an encouraging engineering trade-off. A reader should resist substituting laboratory optical-clock accuracy for the demonstrated device's field stability, or treating equivalent time deviation as measured autonomous positioning. The next useful test would compare against an environmentally independent reference during a longer voyage, report uptime including support equipment, and predefine raw versus corrected holdover metrics. These are proposed validation priorities, not demonstrated defects. The study supports resilient local timing requirements; it does not by itself establish a navigational fix or a network of synchronized clocks. [S10 Figs.1–3; Methods]

## S11 — Demonstration of a mobile optical clock ensemble at sea

Hilton et al.; Nature Communications, 2 July 2025; preprint 6 June 2024. Version of record, full HTML; T1. [Original](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-61140-2).

Three distinct vapour clocks—ytterbium and two rubidium excitation schemes—were compared during the 2022 RIMPAC exercise. Optical beats, microwave differences and RF outputs separate clock noise from counter/reference noise. Three-cornered-hat Modified Allan deviations estimate individual performance. The Yb clock remains in the 10^-14 stability domain from 100 ms to 80 ks; short-time sea performance resembles shore performance, whereas longer-time performance deteriorates. [S11 Portable optical clocks; Clock frequency measurements; Individual clock stabilities; Figs.2–6]

The operational detail qualifies the abstract's unsupervised reliability framing. A deliberate power interruption divided underway records: UofA clocks recovered automatically, while personnel restarted ORAFS. Relocking or configuration changes produced frequency steps. ORAFS's installed ancillary vacuum apparatus makes its actual deployed system 310 kg and 1300 W, larger than the internal physics-package figures. Co-located clocks can acquire correlated environmental noise; the authors explicitly warn that the uncorrelated three-cornered-hat assumption may fail at long times. Data access requires author request and sponsor approval; code is available on request. QuantX ownership and clock IP interests are declared. [S11 Fig.3; Methods Optical rubidium atomic frequency standard; Clock comparisons and analysis; availability/conflict statements]

*Analysis:* The heterogeneous architectures strengthen confidence that vapour clocks can survive motion, but this is the same naval exercise as S10, not an independent environmental replication. It is especially useful because operational steps and support equipment are visible. Blanket claims of superiority should be replaced by a defined timescale and complete installed resource budget. A next campaign should compare uninterrupted holdover through restart events, distinguish frequency continuity from reacquisition, and publish an independent-reference record. Those are application acceptance questions; the published results already justify considering the clocks as timing prototypes. [S11 Figs.3,6–7; Table1; S10 Fig.3]

## S12 — Atom interferometry at arbitrary orientations and rotation rates

d'Armagnac de Castanet et al.; Nature Communications, 30 July 2024; preprint 29 February 2024. Journal HTML and preprint v1 inspected; T1. [Original](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-50804-0).

The paper addresses a concrete moving-platform failure mechanism: rotation separates atomic wave packets and reduces contrast, while rotation and acceleration contribute to the same phase. A rubidium interferometer is hybridized with a classical accelerometer and fibre-optic gyroscopes; a tip-tilt mirror follows real-time commands to preserve overlap. The manual-platform experiment reaches 30-degree tilt and 14 degrees/s rotation, with inferred 24 micro-g per-shot acceleration sensitivity at 20 ms interrogation. [S12 Results; Figs.1–3; Methods Closed-loop mode]

This is one measurement axis, rather than a complete inertial navigator. The rotating fringe SNR is 5.3, compared with 20–30 when static. Reconstruction includes a fitted 1.3% mirror-rate correction. Vibration compensation assumes a flat classical accelerometer response and applies a global 610-microsecond delay; the sensor's bandwidth/self-noise are not precisely characterized, and platform resonances degrade rejection. Full triad operation and long-term stability remain future work. Raw data are deposited as Zenodo 11543715. Several authors are employed by Exail, which commercializes quantum gravimeters; these interests are declared. [S12 Results Rotation compensation; Methods Limits of fringe reconstruction; Discussion; Data availability; Competing interests]

*Analysis:* The defensible advance is preserving and modelling an atomic signal under substantially larger rotations. It should not be described as demonstrated strapdown navigation accuracy, or as removal of classical sensors: those devices are essential to the demonstrated compensation. Its value for UK/EU programmes lies in providing an experimentally testable engineering route and explicit noise limits. Follow-up should evaluate general time-varying three-axis motion, independent characterization of the classical transfer function, and absolute drift in a complete navigator. These are missing deployment checks, not grounds to discard the observed contrast recovery. [S12 Methods; Discussion]

## S13 — Quantum-assured magnetic navigation field trials

Muradoglu et al.; arXiv 2504.08167v1, 10 April 2025; no journal reference found on inspected landing page. Full HTML; T2, not peer reviewed. [Original](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.08167v1).

The experiment combines a rubidium scalar magnetometer, classical vector fluxgate, INS and proprietary online denoising/map matching. Aircraft and van trials use public Australian maps. Table1 is the essential evidence: the 22 m endpoint uses onboard sensing and 3D-velocity aiding; the 46-fold improvement uses outboard sensing and 3D velocity, ending at 112 m. An onboard scalar-airspeed case ends at 515 m over 365 km; the high-altitude onboard example ends at 2 km over 300 km. These are different conditions, not a single interchangeable performance claim. [S13 System architecture; Field trials; Table1; Fig.4]

The reported algorithm is iterative and designed for real-time processing, but reconstructing it from the paper is difficult. The full stack is proprietary; no explicit data/code release statement was found in inspected end matter. All authors are Q-CTRL-affiliated. The paper does not isolate quantum-sensor contribution through a classical scalar-sensor ablation or compare against alternative map-matching algorithms on the same inputs. Best-case 3D-velocity provenance is insufficiently specified for treating that result as complete GNSS denial. The authors acknowledge remaining altitude, dynamic-manoeuvre and ocean-map limits. [S13 Fig.1 caption; Q-CTRL MagNav System; Table1; Discussion]

*Analysis:* The field evidence supports an internally mounted magnetic aid that improves the specified drifting INS. It does not establish superiority to GPS, nor uniquely quantum advantage over competitive magnetic navigation. A credible next comparison would preserve map, trajectory, velocity aid and filtering while changing scalar sensors; publish causal replay inputs and predefined route-wide metrics; and test a withheld geography. Endpoint factors can grow when an unaided baseline drifts, so accuracy and mission-duration distributions deserve priority over the largest ratio. This is a proposed evaluation design, not an allegation that the reported tracks are wrong. [S13 Table1; Figs.4–7]

## S14 — GNSS-free quantum gravity-aided maritime navigation

Everitt et al.; arXiv 2608.25563v1, 26 August 2026; no journal reference found on inspected landing page. Full HTML; T2, not peer reviewed. [Original](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.25563v1).

A hybrid rubidium gravimeter corrects raw Boreas D90 IMU measurements through gravity-map matching. Methods explicitly exclude embedded GNSS-derived position, velocity and attitude from independent inertial mechanization and gravity aiding. GNSS is retained for withheld evaluation. Over the 83 km route, endpoint error is 2.2 nautical miles (about 4.1 km), versus 14 nautical miles for unaided INS; approximately one-nautical-mile error holds over the first 70 km. [S14 Results Quantum navigation; Fig.2; Methods Gravity-aided navigation]

The abstract's DRMS wording and Discussion's approximately one-nautical-mile bound are not fully consistent with the endpoint result; Fig.2 explicitly says its shaded one-mile band is not reported DRMS. The separate surveying branch uses GNSS attitude/velocity and Eötvös correction: its 0.67 mGal stability and 0.16 mGal repeatability cannot be transferred to denied navigation. Coastal strapdown passes were typically Sea State1, whereas gimbaled passes faced Sea States2–3. Same route/instrument therefore does not make matched-motion comparison. Initial-state provenance/uncertainty is unspecified; fully closed long-duration navigation is future work. The 30 L sensor head excludes electronics. No explicit data/code release declaration was found. UK Royal Navy/DASA support is acknowledged. [S14 Abstract; Fig.2; Discussion; Methods Maritime traversals, Quantum sensor head, Surveying; Acknowledgments]

*Analysis:* This is consequential provisional evidence of explicit processing-chain GNSS exclusion. The careful headline is finite-route gravity aiding, not a universal one-mile guarantee or autonomous multiday deployment. Recorded-data processing does not itself establish onboard real-time execution. Priorities are initialization disclosure, causal processing latency, withheld routes/maps, full system resource accounting, and extended comparison against corrected classical gravimetry. The stationary 56-hour drift test is encouraging but does not substitute for the coupled moving-navigation experiment. [S14 Figs.2,6–8; Discussion]

## S15 — High-fidelity quantum sensor modelling and map matching

Lellouch and Holynski; Quantum Science and Technology 10 045007, online 6 August 2025 (Crossref DOI metadata); first preprint 15 April 2025. arXiv v2 of 8 August 2025 reviewed; T1 journal-associated manuscript, version distinction retained. [Original reviewed](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.11119v2), [journal DOI](https://doi.org/10.1088/2058-9565/adf2d9).

The contribution is an end-to-end simulation rather than a navigation trial. A Monte-Carlo atomic-cloud model accounts for coupled internal/external evolution, trajectories and imperfect pulses; its measurements feed a particle map-matching filter and INS model. In Sea State2, moderate/enhanced sensor cases cannot yield useful stable aiding after tuning. A fivefold assumed roll/pitch reduction enables maritime mitigation; aircraft examples require a hundredfold reduction. The appendix derives a 3.3-degree static tilt limit for a specified 10 E gradient-error budget. [S15 §§II–IV; Figs.7–8; Appendix B]

The conditions are substantial: runs begin with transfer alignment, assumed 100 m north/east initialization errors, and filter weighting chosen from observed measurement noise. Maritime segments use separate sea-state tuning. The motion mitigation is an imposed scenario, not an achieved actuator performance. Data and code are available on reasonable request; no public archive is given in the statement. Authors declare no competing interests. The arXiv v1 HTML displayed an anomalous internal 2026 date, so identity uses the arXiv submission history, v2 and DOI publication metadata, rather than that rendered dateline. [S15 §IV; Appendix C; availability/conflict statements; arXiv landing/version history]

*Analysis:* This is a useful adverse result: laboratory sensitivity and Gaussian navigation noise assumptions do not automatically survive motion. Its strongest policy value is exposing a design dependency that can be tested before accepting operational claims. The 3.3-degree number is conditional and should not become a universal platform acceptance threshold. Evaluation should compare simulated non-Gaussian tails with measured dynamic sensor data and include realistic mitigation errors, latency and filter tuning. Until then, bounded simulated tracks support an engineering hypothesis, not deployment readiness. [S15 §§IV–V; Appendix B]

## Verification and gaps

Identity/version, quantitative qualifiers, methods, declared conflicts and actual availability statements were checked against full originals. JSON parses and contains unique S10–S15 IDs, 20 screened records, six full-text records and dated searches. No experiments were reproduced, author data were not requested, supplementary files were not independently reviewed, and no paid access was used. Parent/Astra challenge remains separate and may revise consequential claims. Main uncertainty: reproducible proprietary navigation comparisons and initialization/causal execution details, rather than whether the printed experiments exist.
