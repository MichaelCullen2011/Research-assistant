### S10: Quantum Technology: The Second Quantum Revolution
- **URL / file:** https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/0206091 (full text: https://arxiv.org/pdf/quant-ph/0206091)
- **Author / publisher:** Jonathan P. Dowling, Gerard J. Milburn (arXiv)
- **Published:** 2002-06-13 (arXiv v1) · **Accessed:** 2026-10-10
- **Type / tier:** preprint (review) / T2. The arXiv page does not show peer review. The caller's lead gives the journal version as Phil. Trans. R. Soc. A, 2003, but I did not open that version.
- **Region:** Global (US/Australia authors)
- **Access:** full-text
- **Why it matters:** This is the paper that coined the "second quantum revolution" framing. It sets out the first-revolution examples (semiconductors, photoelectric effect, laser) and lists six quantum principles that quantum technology uses.
- **Key points:**
  - The first revolution came from wave–particle duality. Electron-as-wave explains the electronic wavefunctions that underpin semiconductor physics and the chip industry. Light-as-particle explains the photoelectric effect (solar cells, photocopiers). The photon concept explains the laser. (Intro, p.1–2)
  - In the second revolution, humans engineer "highly unnatural quantum states of our own design". Examples are artificial atoms (quantum dots) and entangled states, applied to computers, communications, sensors and metrology. (Intro)
  - It lists six principles: quantisation, the uncertainty principle, superposition, tunnelling, entanglement and decoherence. (Section "Quantum principles")
  - Research programmes named: quantum information technology, quantum electromechanical systems, coherent quantum electronics, quantum optics, and coherent matter technology (atom lasers, BEC). (Abstract)
  - Entanglement could improve optical interferometry toward a 1/N Heisenberg limit. The paper names LIGO and LISA as candidate users. (Quantum optics section)
- **Verbatim quotes:**
  > "The first quantum revolution gave us new rules that govern physical reality. The second quantum revolution will take these rules and use them to develop new technologies." (Abstract)
  > "Once you realize just how an electron acts like a wave, you now can understand the periodic table, chemical interactions, and electronic wavefunctions that underpin the electronic semiconductor physics." (Intro)
  > "The concept of the photon is just what we need to understand the laser." (Intro)
  > "In the First Quantum Revolution, we used quantum mechanics to understand what already existed." (Intro)
  > "Tunneling; the ability of a particle to be found in spatial regions from which classical mechanics would exclude it." (Quantum principles)
- **Caveats:** The paper is from 2002, so its technology-status claims are stale. Use it only for the conceptual framing.

  - Funded by the German BMBF programme "quantum technologies—from basic research to market" (acknowledgements).
- **Verbatim quotes:**
  > "Our results show that current quantum hardware is not yet competitive for large-scale constrained optimisation, although simplified formulations allow non-trivial valid solutions. Quantum-inspired hardware, by contrast, already supports industry-relevant problem sizes for the assignment-based formulations studied here and therefore provides a practically valuable reference point for near-term industrial optimisation workflows." (§VIII.C Conclusion)
  > "the main lesson is not that one solver paradigm universally dominates, but that successful industrial proof-of-concept work currently depends on restricted problem scope, hardware-aware formulation, and explicit matching of optimisation goals to platform capabilities." (§VIII.B)
- **Caveats:** Preprint. Single problem class and company. The industry-scale baseline is classical heuristics, not a state-of-the-art commercial MILP or constraint-programming solver run at scale, so it does not show the Digital Annealer beats the best classical method.
